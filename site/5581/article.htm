Checklist for first-time demo party visitors
============================================
(L) 2004 by grip/pixelmagic

Below is a list of what to bring to a party to make it a comfortable and enjoyable experience. I will give a detailed list of what to bring and why. Then I'll provide a quick recap of the complete checklist and a shorter checklist if you think you can survive by mooching off your friends. Please note that you CAN go to a party with just your computer and nothing else, but it's the extras that makes the party comfortable and worth visiting. I've been to about eleven parties and organized three of those. To some, that might not seem like a lot, but at least I've managed to iron out the dos and don'ts of partygoing.

Allright, let's get on with the checklist:

- Tickets
If you've paid in advance, don't forget them!

- Money
Enough to pay for party entrance, food, beer, train/bus tickets (if something should go wrong) and party paraphenalia such as t-shirts.

- Computer (as little hardware as possible)
Some people go to demo parties without computers. They claim to have fun. They're lying. However, there's no need in bringing your whole machine park. If you're a PC scener, just bring your PC. Even if you think it would be cool to bring along your old A500 to play some nostalgic games, don't. Many parties are restrictive about both space and electricity. If you don't need your 20" CRT, bring your 14" TFT instead. If you're a PC scener and have a powerful enough laptop, bring that one and leave all other computers at home. If you're an Amiga scener and you only need an A1200D, don't bring your A4000T just because it's cool. Small computers are easier to carry around. It's as simple as that.

- A NIC and a computer to use it in
This is an exception from the above. Most voting is nowadays done over the party network. If you compete with an oldschool machine with no obvious networking capabilities (such as a C=64, a Vic-20 or an unexpanded A1200), even the most primitive laptop with a 10MBit NIC can come in handy. During the voting rush it can be tricky to find a networked computer which you can borrow and vote from. Besides, network access is good for a lot of other things. You can check your email, browse the web for programming reference manuals, download that particular file converter you need so badly or just give your friends at home a live party report over IRC.

- Headphones
Especially if you're a musician. There will be loud music at the party. I have never, ever been to a quiet party.

- Operating system install media and...
- Install media for the basic programs you need to compete
At every demo party at least one computer fucks up. If it's your time to end up in hardware hell, these things are good to bring along. For example, if you're an Amiga 2D pixel artist you might like to bring at least your Workbench disks and a copy of Personal Paint. I also advice you to bring some util disks. My "must-have" Amiga utils are SnoopDOS, LHA, LZX, DMS, PPShow and NComm. Put that on a couple of disks along with your favourite shell and text editor and you can get a usable system up and running in notime.

- Empty floppies and/or empty CDs
See above. For backup purposes (at a real demo party, you don't have time to leech ;-).

- Tools
Even if your hardware doesn't break down, a screwdriver and a knife are the very basics to bring. You might for example need to put one of your hard disks in someone else's computer. I recommend bringing some kind of compact computer toolbox. There are for example cheap screwdrivers with bits in the handle. Combine that with a swiss army knife and you are ready to crudely fix at least some problems.

- AC multipliers
You can never get enough of them!

- Extra TP cables
See above

- Räkost and knäckebröd
Perfect emergency food. At three o'clock in the morning when all the stores are closed, this makes for an excellent bukfylla. Both räkost (for fast carbs and great taste) and knäckebröd (plenty of fibres to bind all that coffee) will stay fresh in room temperature during the whole party.

- Your compo contribs
If you have started - or even finished - your contribs before the party, make sure not to forget them. Trying to recreate all those lines of assembly code before deadline isn't very funny. You _are_ competing, right? Some people go to demo parties without competing. They claim to have fun. They're lying.

- Backup copies of your contribs
Like I said, if your computer fucks up, you'll feel much better when you can at least hand in a CD/floppy and take part in the compo(s).

- Sleeping bag and inflatable matress
It's not nice having to sleep on a table using your coat as a blanket, even if many sceners have done that during their careers. Quality sleep is hard to get with 300dB gabber in one ear and someone discussing the Sega Master System's palette quirks in your other, so you might as well try to make it as comfy for you as possible.

- Painkillers and band-aids
If you get a headache from sleep depravation or cut yourself while bending away the protective metal sheild on your A600 MoBo, things like this might come in handy.

- Toothbrush and toothpaste
The very basics of party hygiene. Even if there are no showers, brushing your teeth will make you feel refreshed and ready for another 24 hours of zombie-like awakenness.

- Soap, deodorant and towel
If you have access to showers at the party, this is an absolute must. Nothing like being able to rid yourself of all that late night computer sweat.

- Clean clothes
Even if you can't shower, you can wash your armpits and put on a nice, fresh t-shirt.

- Funny accessories
Nothing is quite like being able to decorate your table space in an individual manner. This can range from modified and/or otherwise cool-looking hardware to such advanced things as complete group logos made in LEGO or a girl/boy-friend to show off for your geek friends. Whatever floats your boat.

Fast recap:

Money
Computer (as little hardware as possible)
Headphones
A NIC and a computer to use it in
Operating system install media
Install media for the basic programs you need to compete
Empty floppies and/or CDs
Tools
AC multipliers
Extra TP cables
Räkost and knäckebröd
Your compo contribs
Backup copies of your contribs (on CD/floppy)

------
So, you've arrived at the party, spotted your seat, unpacked your goods and rigged up your hardware. Ready to party! "But how?", you might think.

Here's a list of what to do and not to do:

- If you're drunk, don't disturb people who are working on their productions. Of course you can talk to them and ask about what they're doing, but if you're drunk enough to be unable to control yourself, don't go near them. Nobody wants a beer-spilling social freak falling on their keyboard when they're just about to save that last, crucial line of code.

- Don't just sit there and play Quake or leech warez. You can do that at home all the time thanks to Internet, why pay good money to leave your comfy bed only for watching that SAMBA progress bar for three days?

- Don't be rude to people. The scene is about mutual respect. A guy who's struggling with interference circles in TurboPascal is as much a scener as someone who is coding a C++ 3D routine for DirectX 9, and vice versa. We are all there to have fun and watch our prods on the big screen. Most importantly: we all have to begin our careers somewhere.

- Don't boo out a demo just because you think it's ugly. You'll get your chance to speak your mind when voting. Booing might scare away a would-be code/graphics/music genious.

- Socialize and gather inspiration. Be it outside the party hall with beer and drunkenness or inside with code and sobriety. Walk around and check out what people are doing. Many of them will probably be working on their party productions. This is a good chance to get a feeling of the competition in your category.

If you see something interesting, have a chat with that person. There's nothing quite like hearing a passionate assembler coder, pixel artist or chip musician describing their bizarre platform of choice, no matter if it's a ZX Spectrum, a Nintendo Entertainment System or an Amstrad CPC 464.

You have a good chance of getting help by other people at a demo party. As a "thank you", you can always include a special greeting to them in your production (either in a scroller or readme). This applies to all categories: Code, graphics, music, whatever. Just keep in mind that if you see a cool effect and the coder tells you how he does it, do not run away and plagiarize that in your own party contrib. Mutual respect is the key.

Once an effect has been shown at a compo or in a released demo, the visual aspects of it is in the public domain. If you liked the way a phong shaded torus looked in a demo from last month, feel free to use that as a source of inspiration. This is how trends are started in the demo world. Of course you can't just rip the whole design of a demo, but a particular effect or coding trick might suit your next production very well. The key to a successful scene carreer is originality. Be creative! Maybe five years from now you can sit back and smile at the fact that you introduced a new kind of effect, drawing style or even music genre.

- Compete, compete and compete. In a perfect world, every single demo party visitor would compete in at least one category. If you feel that you lack skills or ideas, there are usually "anything goes" categories such as Wild demo, Wild graphics or Wild music. The feeling of seeing your production on the big screen with the whole party watching is unbeatable. Even if you know that you have no chance of winning, it's always very exciting when the compo results are presented.

Going to a demo party when not a single member of you group is competing in any of the categories is like sending your country's national track and field team to the Olympic Games only to tell them "Just sit here and watch the other guys run".

It's called a _demo_ party because you're supposed to demonstrate something. Capisce? ;-)

- Enjoy the compos. This is the best part of the party, especially if you are contributing. Even if you're only interested in the demo compo, respect the other categories as well. Someone who's been struggling with a chipmod on an Atari 520ST for two weeks might not see the fun in you playing a David Hasselhof medley during the tracker compo.

- Take care of yourself. Get enough food, sleep, fresh air and sun. Yes, sun. Believe it or not, but sitting in a dark room for 24 hours straight doesn't make you any less tired. Go outside for an hour or so at noon - you've earned it and you need it.

Don't try to make up a fancy sleep schedual - "2 hours sleep, 3 hours awake, 1 hour sleep, 4 hours awake" or something like that - they always break. Go to sleep when you're tired. A good rule of thumb is to try and sleep a lot the first night of the party. That way you'll usually have enough time to make the deadlines _and_ catch some sleep before the compos. Trying to stay awake during the whole party is just plain stupid. While you might pull it through, you're likely to be at the coherency level of green lettuce at the time of the price ceremony. And after that, the party is over and you have to pack all your stuff and go home. Ever try to carry a computer more than fifteen meters when you haven't slept for 64 hours? If you haven't, I can tell you that a demo party is not a good place to find out how that feels.

Caffeine and sugar might help you stay awake for some time, but in the end you will always crash. The more caffeine, the harder the crash. It's just like with alcohol and hangovers. Get your energy from other sources than Jolt and coffee. A good blend of carbohydrates, proteine, fibres, fat and vitamins is the way to go. Some might call this "pizza and burger" while some might call it "chicken salad with cold rice". If you're like me and enjoy a good pizza, go for the real stuff from a pizzeria. Microwave food is like eating a napkin - it'll take away the hunger, but it tastes like crap and contains no nourishment at all.

With that said, you should of course not deprive yourself from the enjoyment of some good, hot coffee or a respectable amount of ice cold Jolt (perhaps with a twist of lemon?). Just don't overdo it.

- Knowing that you are at a demo party, you might get a strange feeling that you're a part of something bigger, something that reminds you of a very alive and constantly changing world-wide sub culture. It's true. You are. Enjoy the demo scene and good luck at your first demo party!

------

Well, there you have it. I hope this little text can help all first-time party goers to make their experience more enjoyable.
